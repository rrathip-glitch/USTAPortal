# USTA Tournament Intelligence Platform — Engineering Specification

Status: Draft v0.1 (pre-recon). Owner: Orchestrator agent. Last meaningful update: see `CHANGELOG.md`.
This document is the canonical engineering spec for the USTA Tournament Intelligence Platform, a personal tool built for a single junior competitor (Janav Thasen) and his support team. It is expected to drift as `RESEARCH.md` and `RECON.md` resolve open unknowns; that drift is described and managed in Section 11.

---

## 1. Vision and Product Goal

The USTA's `playtennis.usta.com` portal is the system of record for sanctioned junior tennis in the United States, but it is not designed for the people who actually have to *use* it competitively. A player and their parents preparing for a weekend tournament face a flat, paginated UI in which every piece of opponent context — the opponent's section, recent match results, current ranking, World Tennis Number (WTN) for both singles and doubles, head-to-head with the player — sits behind several tab-clicks per opponent, repeated for every line in every draw. There is no historical view; once a tournament closes, the draw page becomes harder to retrieve, and there is no place where a parent or coach can see, in one screen, "for the draw he is in this Saturday, here is everyone he could possibly play, sorted by threat, with WTN deltas and prior history." The portal also has no concept of personal trajectory: ranking and WTN snapshots are visible only as the current value, not as a curve.

The product is an offline-first dashboard that mirrors the relevant slice of USTA portal data into a local SQLite database, layers computed intelligence on top, and becomes the canonical interface the user actually opens. After a sync, the user should be able to detach entirely from `playtennis.usta.com` for any read-only workflow: pre-tournament briefing, post-match analysis, ranking trajectory, head-to-head lookup, draw-strength comparison across tournaments. The portal remains authoritative for write actions (entering tournaments, withdrawing, paying fees), but every read happens locally.

Concretely, success is defined as: a user can open the dashboard the night before a tournament, click into the relevant draw, and within thirty seconds have a clear picture of every potential opponent up to the final, ranked by threat, with current WTN for singles and doubles, current section/national ranking with a 12-month trend, last eight match results, and head-to-head if any prior matches exist. The product also requires a complete cached profile, including singles and doubles WTN, for *every player in every draw the user is entered in* — not just the user's projected path. Opponent intelligence is the differentiating value of the system, and it is the first thing that breaks if the scrape is incomplete or shallow.

Explicit non-goals for v1, recorded so that scope creep can be rejected by reference: no betting or odds production; no public sharing or multi-tenant mode (single user, optionally shared read-only with coach/parent via a private URL in a later phase); no machine-learned models in v1 (any "expected outcome" number is an explainable Elo-style rating with a visible derivation, not a black box); no live in-match telemetry or point-by-point capture (the USTA portal does not expose it and we are not bringing in third-party scoring feeds); no reverse-direction write integration (we never click "withdraw" or "enter" on the user's behalf); no general-purpose scraping framework — this tool serves one user's competitive workflow and is not generalized until there is reason to.

A further non-functional goal is resilience to USTA UI changes. The portal is a SPA on top of an enterprise CMS stack and has historically rotated DOM structure without notice. The system must be designed so that a UI change on USTA's side never destroys historical data and rarely blocks a sync for more than an evening's parser fix. The mechanism for this — raw-response caching as the canonical source of truth, with parsers as a separate, replaceable layer — is described in Section 6 and is the single most important architectural commitment in this document.

## 2. Prior Art Mandate

Before any production code beyond stubs is written, the Agent-Research subagent is responsible for surveying prior art and committing findings to `RESEARCH.md`. The spec commits to integrating those findings before Phase 1 application code begins; if Research surfaces, for example, an existing scraper that has already mapped the USTA login flow, we should not re-derive that map by hand.

The investigative axes Research is expected to cover, named here so the surface is not lost: existing USTA scrapers on GitHub (search terms: "playtennis usta", "usta tournament scraper", "tennislink scraper", "world tennis number api", "usta junior rankings", and the older "tennislink" rebrand which still appears in third-party tools); commercial and community tennis stat platforms whose data shape is informative even if not directly reusable (UTR / Universal Tennis, TennisAbstract, Tennis Explorer, Tennis Analytics, ATP/WTA stat APIs, ITF IPIN portal); reverse-engineering writeups for SPA-style sports sites that share the USTA stack pattern (ASP.NET / Sitecore / React hydration) — these tend to share anti-bot and pagination idioms; common anti-bot defenses on enterprise .NET stacks (Akamai Bot Manager, Cloudflare Bot Fight Mode, PerimeterX/HUMAN, Imperva, custom JS challenges, header fingerprinting, TLS fingerprinting via JA3); and the public/private API surface of the World Tennis Number system, which is jointly operated by ITF and surfaces in multiple federation portals — the WTN endpoint may be shared infrastructure and may already be documented in another federation's leaks or community docs.

Research output goes into `RESEARCH.md` with concrete links and short summaries; anything that materially changes architecture or scope is escalated to `QUESTIONS.md` so the Orchestrator can fold it into a decision. The spec does not duplicate Research's findings — when in conflict, the most recent `RESEARCH.md` wins, and this section is updated in the next self-improvement loop.

## 3. Multi-Agent Coordination Protocol

The project is built by a small fleet of cooperating agents — an Orchestrator and several named subagents (Agent-Research, Agent-Spec, Agent-Recon, Agent-Fetch, Agent-Parse, Agent-Enrich, Agent-UI, Agent-Test, Agent-Ops). The full topology and the responsibilities of each agent live in `AGENTS.md`; this section describes only the coordination protocol that lets them work without stepping on each other.

State lives in a known directory of markdown files at the repo root. `STATE.md` is the live "what is happening right now" file: which agent holds the floor, what task they claimed, when they started, and what the next handoff is. `DECISIONS.md` is an append-only ledger of architecture decision records (ADRs), each with a number, a date, a status (proposed/accepted/superseded), and a short rationale; ADR-001 is reserved for the extraction-strategy decision described in Section 4. `QUESTIONS.md` holds open questions whose answers would change the spec, the data model, or the UX — agents add to it freely and the Orchestrator clears it. `TODO.md` is a flat list of concrete next actions, each tagged with the agent expected to do it. `RECON.md` is the structured output of Phase 0 and is owned by Agent-Recon. `RESEARCH.md` is owned by Agent-Research. `DATA_MODEL.md` documents the canonical entity shapes and lives in sync with the Pydantic models and SQLite schema. `API_CONTRACTS.md` records the USTA endpoints we depend on (URL, method, params, observed response shape, last-verified date). `RUNBOOK.md` is the operational playbook described in Section 10. `TESTING.md` is the testing strategy described in Section 9, kept as a living document because canary findings continually update it. `AGENTS.md` defines the agent fleet. `CHANGELOG.md` is the human-readable log of meaningful sessions, written at the end of each session.

The protocol every agent follows on every run: read `STATE.md` first, top to bottom. If another agent currently holds the floor, either wait, or, if the new task is independent, write a claim line declaring the new task and the working directory subset being touched. Do the work. Update the state files that materially changed (`DECISIONS.md` if an ADR was made, `QUESTIONS.md` if a question was opened or closed, `TODO.md` if items moved, `DATA_MODEL.md` or `API_CONTRACTS.md` if those moved, the relevant agent-owned doc). Append a `CHANGELOG.md` entry. Release the floor in `STATE.md` and write the recommended next action. The "every agent reads STATE first" rule is non-negotiable and is enforced by a session-start hook on Claude Code on the web.

Escalation: any time an agent encounters a question whose answer would meaningfully change architecture, data model, or UX, the agent does not silently choose — it writes the question to `QUESTIONS.md` with context and a tentative recommendation, and (if blocking) pauses. The Orchestrator resolves it, either directly or by spawning a research task, and records the resolution as an ADR in `DECISIONS.md` if it is durable. This section cross-references Section 11, which describes how the same files feed the self-improvement loop that keeps this spec honest.

## 4. Reconnaissance Phase

**Pivot note (2026-05-10).** Live recon confirmed `playtennis.usta.com` (and the rest of the Clubspark edge) is Cloudflare-blocked at IP/ASN layer from every egress available to this dev environment; ADR-001 stands as Accepted (Strategy C) but the Clubspark data plane is deferred until the user re-runs recon from a residential egress (Q-011). In the meantime, `tennislink.usta.com` — the legacy ASP.NET surface — is reachable from this environment and becomes the **primary data source** for v1. The fetch layer was refactored into a multi-source design (`FetchRouter` with TennisLink and Clubspark drivers) captured in ADR-005; the rest of this section is the original recon charter, still applicable to whichever source we're attacking.

Reconnaissance is mandatory before Phase 1 application code begins, beyond the lightweight stubs needed to prove the agent harness works. The reason is that the entire shape of the fetch and auth layers depends on facts about `playtennis.usta.com` that we do not know at the time of this draft, and guessing them produces a fetch layer that has to be rewritten the moment recon finishes. Phase 0, executed by Agent-Recon in a session that has access to working USTA credentials, produces `RECON.md`, fills the initial draft of `API_CONTRACTS.md`, and resolves ADR-001.

The investigative surface, in priority order. The auth flow: is login a classical form POST that sets a session cookie, or is it OIDC against a federated identity provider (the USTA has historically used Okta and Auth0 in different surfaces), or is it a hybrid where a CMS-side cookie is exchanged for an API bearer token? What are the cookie names? Is there a CSRF token tied to the session, and where is it injected (meta tag, response header, hidden form input)? What is the refresh behavior — silent refresh on a timer, refresh on 401, or hard re-login? Does the auth surface expose multi-factor and, if so, can a session be persisted long enough that MFA is rare?

Internal APIs: with DevTools' Network panel filtered to XHR/Fetch, walking through every screen the user cares about — dashboard, my tournaments, tournament page, draw page, player profile, rankings, WTN — what endpoints are called? Do they return JSON, server-rendered HTML fragments, or hydrated React payloads (`__NEXT_DATA__`-style or similar)? What is the auth header pattern (cookie-only, bearer token, both)? Are there obvious internal API base URLs, and are they versioned?

SPA framework: is the front end React, Angular, Vue, or server-rendered with progressive hydration? The framework matters because it changes how we extract data from a rendered page if internal APIs turn out to be insufficient. A React SPA typically exposes a well-shaped JSON store somewhere in the DOM or in a `window.__INITIAL_STATE__` global, which is far easier to parse than scraping the rendered HTML.

Anti-bot measures: is there a Cloudflare or Akamai challenge page on first request from a clean IP? Are there JS challenges that require a real browser to solve? Is there TLS fingerprinting (httpx may need to be replaced with `curl_cffi` or the request must be tunneled through Playwright)? Are there captchas that appear under sustained load, and at what request rate do they trigger? Does the site appear to fingerprint the browser (canvas, WebGL, audio, font enumeration)? These answers determine whether we can ever run a pure-httpx fetch path.

WTN access pathway: WTN is the single most product-critical field. Is it exposed inline in the player profile API response, or is it a separate authenticated call to a different endpoint (possibly under an ITF-shared domain)? Is doubles WTN equally accessible to singles WTN, or gated differently? Does WTN visibility require the viewer to be logged in, and if so does it require any specific entitlement (e.g., USTA membership tier)?

Identifier formats: the example URL `https://playtennis.usta.com/Competitions/tritennis0/Tournaments/draws/CB005855-CDEF-4A4A-8885-4D3A52C9B413` contains a GUID. Is this the tournament ID, the draw ID, or something else? Are player IDs GUIDs as well, or numeric, or both (a GUID for the API and a numeric for legacy URLs)? Are tournament IDs stable across years, or generated per-instance? Pagination: do list endpoints return a continuation token, an offset, or do they slam everything in one response? Schema-drift signals: are there obvious version markers in the API responses, build hashes in JS bundles, or release notes anywhere visible that we can hook into?

The how, in order of preference. First, browser DevTools with Network open and "Preserve log" on, performing a manual login and walking every screen of interest, exporting the HAR. This is the cheapest source of truth and the only one that captures the *intended* flow. Second, Playwright in headed mode with a network-interception script that captures every request and response into a session log on disk; this gives us a reproducible record we can re-analyze. Third, static analysis of the SPA bundle — pulling the JS, running it through a deobfuscator, grepping for API base URL constants, route definitions, and feature-flag strings. Fourth, only after the auth shape is fully understood, direct HTTP with copied session cookies to confirm endpoints behave as expected when called outside the browser; this is the riskiest step and is gated on the prior three.

ADR-001 "Extraction Strategy" is the decision gate that ends Phase 0. It must resolve to one of three options. Option (a): the site exposes clean internal JSON APIs, in which case the fetch layer is httpx with replayed auth, and Playwright is used only for the login dance and the occasional session refresh. Option (b): the data is server-rendered, or only exposed inside a hydrated React tree that requires the page to be evaluated, in which case Playwright is the primary fetch tool and we extract from the rendered DOM or from `window`-globals. Option (c): the data is reachable via internal API but only with active session warming (e.g., short-lived bearer tokens minted by a JS challenge), in which case Playwright maintains a long-lived browser context and httpx makes the actual data calls through that warmed session. The ADR records the rationale, the evidence, and the implications for rate limits and reliability. Subsequent ADRs build on it.

## 5. Data Model

The data model is described here as prose, walking each canonical entity in turn. The full field list and SQL schema live in `DATA_MODEL.md`, which moves in lockstep with the Pydantic v2 models in `models/`. This section is the *narrative*, not the authoritative schema.

The **Player** is the central entity. A player has a USTA ID (treated as the primary key — names collide, particularly in junior tennis where multiple players share a name within a section), full name, gender, the section and district they compete out of, their age category (12s, 14s, 16s, 18s, plus open and adult categories where relevant), current rankings indexed by category and scope (section, national, sometimes district), singles WTN, doubles WTN, profile URL on the USTA site, and a `last_fetched_at` timestamp that drives the staleness display in the UI. WTN is stored both as the current value and as a time series (see WTNSnapshot below).

The **Tournament** entity has a USTA ID, name, level (a controlled vocabulary: L1-L7 in junior tennis plus various adult and senior tiers — exact ladder confirmed in recon), sanctioning body (USTA Section, USTA National, ITF, etc.), start and end dates, location (venue, city, state), surface (hard, clay, indoor, grass), ball type (regular, green dot, orange ball, etc., relevant for younger age groups), divisions offered, entry deadline, and status (upcoming, in progress, complete, cancelled). Tournaments do not own players directly; they own draws, and draws own entries.

The **Draw** entity belongs to a tournament. A draw has a name (typically the division descriptor, e.g., "Boys' 18 Singles Main Draw"), a format (single elimination, round robin, compass, consolation, hybrid — and "hybrid" is common in lower levels where round robins feed elimination brackets), size (typically a power of two for elimination, but variable for round robin and compass), gender, age group, division, and status. The format determines how matches relate to one another and is therefore parsed and validated, not free text.

A **DrawEntry** is a player's place in a draw. It has a draw FK, a player FK, a seed (nullable — most entrants are unseeded), a position (the line in the bracket; for elimination this is often called the "line number"), and a status (entered, withdrawn before play, walkover-in to round 1, walkover-out, etc.). Entries are soft-deleted, never hard-deleted: a withdrawn player is preserved with status set, because re-running a sync against a now-empty line is otherwise indistinguishable from a parse miss, and historically the user wants to know who *was* in a draw before late withdrawals.

The **Match** entity belongs to a draw, has a round (R128, R64, R32, R16, QF, SF, F, plus consolation rounds and round-robin round numbers), a scheduled time (nullable — many matches are not scheduled until the morning of), a court assignment (nullable until on-site), player1 and player2 FKs (nullable when a bye or a TBD remains), a raw score string as it appears on the USTA site, a parsed score (set list, tiebreak details, retirement/walkover flag), a winner FK, and a `last_fetched_at`. Matches are stored even before they are played, with player FKs filled in as the bracket resolves; this is what powers the "projected path" view.

The **RankingSnapshot** entity records a player's ranking at a point in time: player FK, category (e.g., Boys 16 Singles), scope (section, national, district), position, points (where exposed), and `as_of` date. WTN is parallel: a **WTNSnapshot** records player FK, type (singles or doubles), value, confidence (WTN exposes a confidence signal), and `as_of`. Both snapshots are append-only and form the basis of the trajectory views.

Derived entities, computed from the above and rebuildable at any time. **HeadToHead** is a logical view rather than a stored entity in v1: given two player FKs, the system queries every match in which both appear and produces a record. It is materialized into a cache table for performance once the dataset gets large. **FormWindow** is similar: for a player and a window definition (last N matches, or last K days), compute a win-loss record and a quality-adjusted record that weights wins over higher-rated opponents more heavily. **StrengthOfDraw** is per-(draw, player) and answers "for this draw, how hard is this player's projected path on average and at peak"; it produces an average opponent rating, the hardest projected opponent up to the final, and a description of the easiest path. **ExpectedOutcome** is per-match and stores a win probability per side along with the model version that produced it; v1 uses a transparent Elo derivation rather than ML.

Two normalization rules pervade the schema. First, USTA ID is the primary key for players — the system never resolves players by name, and a fetch that returns a name without a USTA ID is treated as a parse failure. Second, the **raw-response cache** is the source of truth: every fetch writes the unparsed response to `data/raw/<endpoint>/<sha256-of-(endpoint, params)>.<ext>` along with a small metadata sidecar (`fetched_at`, `request`, `response_status`, `response_headers`). Parsers read from cache in normal operation, not from the wire. SQLite contents are derived state and can be rebuilt from raw cache by re-running the parsers. This is the single concrete mechanism that delivers the resilience-to-UI-change non-functional goal stated in Section 1.

## 6. Architecture

The architecture is a conventional layered design, described top-down. Each layer is a Python package under `usta_portal/` and depends only on the layer below it. The diagram below sketches the topology; the prose underneath explains the responsibilities and the orchestration sequence that ties the layers together at sync time.

```
+------------------------------------------------------------+
|  UI            (FastAPI routes + Jinja2 templates)         |
+------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Enrichment    (h2h, form, strength-of-draw, elo)          |
+------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Storage       (SQLite via repositories.py)                |
+------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Parse         (entity-typed parsers; source-aware)        |
+------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Fetch.Router  (FetchRouter — dispatches to a source)      |
|     |-- TennisLinkClient  (primary today; ASP.NET HTML)    |
|     `-- ClubsparkClient   (deferred stub — gated on Q-011) |
|  Fetch.Client  (generic httpx: rate-limit, retry, cache)   |
+------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Auth          (session lifecycle, cookie jar / Playwright)|
+------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Raw cache     (data/raw/<two-hex>/<sha256-hex>.<ext>)     |
+------------------------------------------------------------+
```

The **Auth** layer owns the session lifecycle: log in, persist the session (cookie jar plus any bearer token and expiry), refresh proactively, and surface `AuthExpiredError` cleanly to higher layers. Whether the layer drives Playwright or pure httpx depends on the source — TennisLink today is reachable with plain httpx (it sets `ASP.NET_SessionId` and an `AntiCsrfTokenTL` cookie that we replay), while Clubspark requires Playwright per ADR-001. The auth state is a small finite-state machine (logged out, logging in, authenticated, refreshing, locked-out) with explicit transitions, so that retries and backoff have something concrete to interrogate.

The **Fetch** layer is split into a generic transport (`FetchClient` in `src/fetch/client.py` — rate limit, retry/backoff, raw-cache writer, header redaction) and a multi-source router (`FetchRouter` in `src/fetch/router.py`). The router exposes the *entity-level* surface that the orchestrator calls: `get_player(id)`, `get_tournament(id)`, `get_draw(id)`. It dispatches to one of two source-specific clients per ADR-005:

- `TennisLinkClient` (`src/fetch/tennislink_client.py`) — primary today. Talks to `tennislink.usta.com`, an ASP.NET WebForms surface that is *not* Cloudflare-fronted and is reachable from datacenter egress. Returns raw HTML; parsers in `src/parse/` typed-extract from it.
- `ClubsparkClient` (`src/fetch/clubspark_client.py`) — deferred stub. The Strategy-C target from ADR-001; each method raises `NotImplementedError` until residential egress is available (Q-011). The router catches that exception, logs cleanly, and falls through to TennisLink.

Source dispatch logic: the router honors a configured preference order (default `tennislink,clubspark`, overridable via `USTA_SOURCE_PREFERENCE`), with a heuristic exception — if the entity id is a Clubspark-shaped GUID (`8-4-4-4-12` hex) the router tries Clubspark first since the caller clearly has Clubspark data. On `BlockedEgressError` (Cloudflare 403 on Clubspark) or `NotImplementedError`, the router falls through to the next source; on any other error it propagates to the orchestrator. Rate limits live in the underlying `FetchClient` — default one request per two seconds, jittered, respecting `Retry-After`.

The **Parse** layer turns raw responses into typed entities. Parsers are structured per endpoint and per entity, are pure functions of the raw input, and never perform IO. This is what enables the "reparse from cache" recovery story: after a parser bug fix, we run `usta-portal reparse` and rebuild the SQLite database from the existing raw cache without hitting the network. Parse errors are typed (`ParseError`, with `SchemaDriftError` as a subclass for the case where the response shape has shifted in a way that suggests USTA changed the page), so that the schema-drift canary in Section 9 can react meaningfully.

The **Storage** layer is repositories on top of SQLite. Each entity has a repository with `get_by_id`, `upsert`, `query` methods; the repositories own the SQL and the migration story. SQLAlchemy 2.x or sqlite-utils — the choice is recorded as ADR-002 once Phase 1 begins, with a default to sqlite-utils for simplicity unless the relational queries get hairy.

The **Enrichment** layer computes the derived intelligence: head-to-head records, form windows, strength-of-draw, Elo-style expected outcomes. Enrichments are deterministic, drillable (every aggregate exposes the underlying matches), and idempotent — running enrichment twice on the same data produces the same output.

The **UI** layer is FastAPI with Jinja2 templates. The choice of FastAPI + Jinja2 over Streamlit is justified in Section 8.

The **sync orchestration sequence**, executed by `usta-portal sync`, is: identify the user's tournaments (the user's player profile lists upcoming and recent tournament entries); for each tournament, fetch the tournament detail to enumerate draws; for each draw, fetch entries; for each entry, fetch the player profile (which is assumed to include WTN — see Section 5 for the assumption marker, and Section 4 for the recon finding that resolves it); fetch matches as they appear in the draw; compute enrichments at the end. Each step writes to the raw cache and to SQLite; each step is independently re-runnable.

Errors are typed so that the orchestrator can branch correctly: `AuthExpiredError` triggers a single re-auth and one retry; `BotChallengeError` triggers a long backoff and surfaces to the user (it usually means we are being rate-limited or have hit a captcha); `RateLimitedError` honors `Retry-After`; `TransientNetworkError` retries with exponential backoff up to a cap; `ParseError` writes the offending raw response path into the error log and continues with other entities; `SchemaDriftError` additionally pings the schema-drift canary; `EntityNotFoundError` is a normal terminal — it means a draw was deleted or a player was removed, and the orchestrator records it and moves on.

Rate limiting and politeness defaults reflect that this is a personal-use tool that should never approximate adversarial behavior. The default request interval is two seconds with jitter; concurrent requests are not used unless ADR documents a need; sustained sync against a dozen tournaments at this rate completes well within an evening. The README documents the ToS posture explicitly: this is a single user fetching their own tournament participation data at human-comparable rates, and the user is required to acknowledge that USTA's terms govern their use of the portal and that this tool exists only to make their own data legible to them.

## 7. Intelligence Layer

The intelligence layer is what makes this a *dashboard* rather than a *mirror*. The primary view is the **pre-tournament briefing**: a single page that, for an upcoming draw the user is entered in, renders the user's projected path through the bracket; for each potential opponent at each round, an opponent scouting card; and an aggregate strength-of-draw figure that compares this draw to peer draws (other 16s draws at the same tournament, other tournaments the user has played).

The **opponent scouting card** is the core unit. It contains the opponent's full name, age category, section and district, current ranking (with the relevant scope auto-selected — section if the tournament is sectional, national if national), a 12-month ranking trend rendered as a sparkline, singles and doubles WTN, recent results in a window (default last eight matches, with score, opponent name, opponent WTN at time of match, and tournament context), head-to-head with the user (record, last meeting, last score), common opponents with comparative scorelines (the user beat X 6-2 6-3, this opponent lost to X 4-6 3-6, suggesting an edge), and surface preference if inferable from the win-loss split across surfaces in the player's history. The card is dense by design — a coach should be able to read it in under five seconds.

The **user self-view** is the second pillar: ranking trajectory across the relevant categories, WTN trajectory for singles and doubles, win-loss split by surface, by round, and by seed status of opponent (vs. seeds, vs. unseeded), and a "performance vs. expectations" view that flags wins over higher-WTN opponents and losses to lower-WTN opponents.

A few computational principles run through the layer. All computations are deterministic — given the same SQLite snapshot, the same numbers come out. All computations are drillable — clicking a stat opens the underlying match list that produced it, so a parent or coach can audit the number rather than trust it. No machine learning in v1: an "expected outcome" probability, where shown, is a transparent Elo-style rating with a documented K-factor and a visible derivation; the rationale is that an opaque ML model the user cannot audit is worse than a simple model the user can. Results are staleness-marked: every aggregate carries the maximum `last_fetched_at` of its inputs, and the UI displays a banner if the underlying data is older than a configurable threshold (default 24 hours for an upcoming-tournament view, 7 days for a historical view).

## 8. UI

The UI is FastAPI with Jinja2 templates as the default. Streamlit was considered explicitly and rejected as the default for three reasons: Railway hosting works smoothly with a uvicorn process and the user has prior art deploying FastAPI on Railway, which de-risks the deploy story; Streamlit's auth and multi-page story is weaker than a server-rendered app where each route is just a function; and a server-rendered approach gives clean shareable URLs (a parent or coach gets a link to a specific draw page rather than a Streamlit session). Streamlit may return as an analyst-mode tool in Phase 5 for ad-hoc exploration, but it is not the user-facing surface.

The pages, enumerated. The root `/` is the dashboard: the user's next tournament with the briefing teaser, recent results, current WTN with delta from last week, current ranking with delta. `/tournaments` is the list of all tournaments the user is or has been in. `/tournaments/{id}` is the detail view with all draws in that tournament. `/draws/{id}` is the bracket view, and crucially renders opponent cards inline for every player in the draw, not just for the user's projected path — this is the screen the user actually opens before a tournament. `/players/{id}` is the standalone scouting card for any player, which is what a coach uses when scouting outside the user's draws. `/h2h/{a}/{b}` is the head-to-head deep dive. `/sync` is the manual sync trigger, with status and the last-sync timestamps. `/health` is the Railway healthcheck. `/admin/raw/{hash}` is the raw cache inspector for debugging — given a request signature hash, it returns the cached raw response, which is invaluable when investigating a parse failure.

The offline-first principle: every page renders entirely from local SQLite plus the raw cache. No page blocks on a USTA request. Sync is a discrete, user-initiated action (or a scheduled background job), and the dashboard works offline once a sync has occurred.

## 9. Testing Strategy

Tests follow a pyramid that reflects the actual cost and value at each layer. The full strategy is maintained in `TESTING.md` because the canary findings continually update it; this section is the durable framing.

**Unit tests** cover parsers, enrichment math, the rate-limiter, and the auth state machine. Parsers are tested by feeding them fixture raw responses and asserting on entity output. Enrichment math is tested with hand-constructed match histories. The rate-limiter and auth state machine are tested as pure state transitions. All unit tests are fast (sub-second per file), perform no IO beyond reading test fixtures, and run on every commit.

**Integration tests** cover parse-from-fixture pipelines (raw response → parsed entity → repository write → repository read), and sync-orchestrator runs with the fetch layer mocked. These are slower and run on PR.

**Smoke tests** run the full pipeline against a captured raw-response set for one known draw — typically a draw the user has played in, fully captured at a known good moment — and assert that the pipeline still produces the expected briefing. The smoke test is the canary that catches "we changed something deep and now nothing works."

Fixtures are anonymized. Every raw response committed to `tests/fixtures/` passes through a redactor that swaps real USTA IDs for synthetic ones from a stable mapping, and zeroes out names and emails of non-public minor players (the user's own data is not redacted in his personal fork, but anything that ships in the public repo is). The redactor itself is tested — it must be idempotent (redacting twice gives the same output) and it must produce parseable output (a redacted fixture is still consumable by the parser).

**Schema-drift detection** runs nightly. A small canary script re-fetches a single known endpoint, diffs the response shape against the last-known schema, and writes a report; if fields appear or disappear, the canary opens a `QUESTIONS.md` entry tagged `schema-drift` and the next agent run picks it up. The canary does not auto-update the parsers — schema changes are decisions, not auto-applied patches.

**Property tests** on score parsing: parsing a score string and reformatting it must round-trip; a parsed set must obey tennis scoring rules (no 7-4 sets without a tiebreak marker, tiebreaks must reach seven with a two-game margin or follow super-tiebreak rules where applicable, retirements and walkovers carry the right flags).

Coverage targets: 90% on `parse/`, `enrich/`, `models/` (these are pure and easy to cover); 70% on `fetch/` and `auth/` (these are IO-bound and harder); the integration smoke must pass before any commit to main, enforced in CI.

## 10. Operational Runbook

The operational runbook lives in `RUNBOOK.md` and is summarized here. Common operations: `usta-portal sync` performs a full sync; `usta-portal sync --tournament <id>` force-resyncs a single tournament; `usta-portal reparse` rebuilds the SQLite database from the raw cache without touching the network and is the recovery tool of choice after a parser fix; `usta-portal export draw <id> --format json` exports a draw for external analysis; credential rotation is `usta-portal auth login` followed by re-running sync; `usta-portal raw show <hash>` prints a cached raw response for inspection.

The troubleshooting tree is the single most useful artifact for the user. **Auth fails**: check that `USTA_USERNAME` and `USTA_PASSWORD` are set; check whether USTA changed the login flow by manually logging in with DevTools open and comparing the request shape against `API_CONTRACTS.md`; check for a new bot challenge (a captcha screen on login is the most common mode of failure for this class of tool); if the login flow changed, an ADR is likely needed and the auth layer must be updated. **Bot challenge** mid-sync: back off, lower the rate, switch to Playwright if currently httpx-only (this is the Section 4 ADR-001 escape hatch), consider browser fingerprint spoofing in the Playwright launch options, and if challenges persist, sync less often. **Parse errors**: run the schema-drift canary, identify the changed fields, update the parser, and run `usta-portal reparse` to recover the affected entities from the raw cache without re-fetching. **WTN missing**: confirm the endpoint still returns it (raw cache inspector); check whether USTA has gated WTN behind a different scope or a separate call (this is the Section 4 recon question that may flip); update fetch and parse layers accordingly. **Slow sync**: profile fetch versus parse — fetch is almost always the bottleneck, but parse can become slow on large tournament fields; check the rate limit settings; consider parallelizing across distinct tournaments (with caution, since parallel requests to the same host can trip anti-bot). **DB locked** errors: SQLite write contention, almost always a crashed sync still holding a lock; ensure single-writer discipline (the sync worker is the only writer; the FastAPI process is read-only); check for stale lock files.

Recovery from total failure is the design payoff. Because the raw cache is the canonical source of truth, if SQLite is corrupted or destroyed, `usta-portal reparse` rebuilds every derived entity from `data/raw/`. As long as the Railway persistent volume holding `data/` survives, no historical data is lost. The Railway volume is the single thing whose backup matters, and the runbook includes a documented `rsync` of `data/raw/` and `data/usta_portal.sqlite` to a separate location on a schedule.

Monitoring is light. The `/health` endpoint returns `{status, db, last_sync}` for Railway to monitor; a `last_sync` older than the configured staleness threshold returns a degraded status (still 200 so Railway does not restart the process — this is a known gotcha). The dashboard header surfaces sync staleness directly so the user notices before the data goes stale enough to matter.

## 11. Self-Improvement Loop

This is the heart of why we have an `AGENTS.md` and a fleet of subagents in the first place. After every meaningful work session — a feature added, recon completed, a bug fixed, a schema-drift canary alert resolved — the Orchestrator runs `scripts/update_canonical_docs.py`. The script scans `CHANGELOG.md`, the recently modified files, and `STATE.md`, then asks four questions of the canonical docs. Does `SPEC.md` still describe reality, or has the implementation diverged? Did this session resolve any open `QUESTIONS.md` entries (and if so, are the resolutions captured in `DECISIONS.md` as ADRs)? Did this session create new questions that haven't been logged? Were any existing ADRs invalidated or superseded by the work just done, and are there `TODO.md` items now done that should be closed?

The script does not silently rewrite docs. It produces a diff for review — proposed insertions, deletions, and rewordings — that the Orchestrator (or a human) accepts, edits, or rejects. The reason for the human-in-the-loop is that doc drift is meaningful information; if the script is wrong about a divergence, that wrongness is itself a signal worth surfacing rather than papering over.

Subagents follow the same protocol on every run: read state, do work, update state, leave a `CHANGELOG.md` entry. Over time, `SPEC.md` is expected to drift from this initial draft as recon resolves unknowns, the data model fills in, and product use surfaces missing requirements. That drift is the point. The version of this spec at any given commit reflects the team's best current understanding — not a frozen requirements document handed down at project start. Section 3's coordination protocol and this section's self-improvement loop are the two halves of the same contract: agents communicate through state files, and state files are kept honest by the loop.

## 12. Railway Deployment

Railway is the target host. The deploy is built with Nixpacks unless ADR-003 records a switch to a Dockerfile.

The Nixpacks config bundles Python 3.11 plus the system dependencies Playwright Chromium needs: chromium itself, plus the standard set of shared libraries that the headless browser links against — libnss3, libatk1.0-0, libatk-bridge2.0-0, libcups2, libdrm2, libxkbcommon0, libxcomposite1, libxdamage1, libxfixes3, libxrandr2, libgbm1, libpango-1.0-0, libcairo2, libasound2. These are exactly the dependencies the Playwright project documents for its Chromium build; missing any one of them produces an opaque "Browser failed to start" error that is painful to debug, so they are pinned in the Nixpacks config. Post-install runs `playwright install chromium` to download the matching browser binary into the build image.

The Procfile defines two processes. `web` runs `uvicorn usta_portal.app:app --host 0.0.0.0 --port $PORT`. `worker` runs the sync loop, which is a long-lived process that wakes on a schedule, executes a sync, and sleeps. The web and worker processes share the SQLite database via the persistent volume; SQLite is single-writer, so the worker is the only process that opens the database read-write, and the web process opens it read-only.

The persistent volume is mounted at `/data`. Without it, every Railway redeploy nukes the SQLite database and the raw cache, which would defeat the entire offline-first design. The volume must be created manually in the Railway dashboard before the first deploy — Railway's API does not currently allow programmatic volume creation in all plans — and the runbook calls this out explicitly.

Environment variables: `USTA_USERNAME` and `USTA_PASSWORD` for the auth flow; `USTA_USER_PLAYER_ID` for the player whose tournaments are synced (this is the user's own USTA player ID, which the system uses as the entry point to discover everything else); `DATABASE_URL` (default `sqlite:////data/usta_portal.sqlite`); `RAW_CACHE_DIR` (default `/data/raw`); `LOG_LEVEL` (default `INFO`); `REQUEST_INTERVAL_SECONDS` (default `2.0`); `PLAYWRIGHT_HEADLESS` (default `true`); `PORT` (set by Railway); `ENVIRONMENT` (`production` on Railway, `local` otherwise — toggles a few sanity-check guards).

The `/health` endpoint returns 200 with a JSON payload `{status, db, last_sync}` that Railway scrapes for liveness; degraded states return 200 with `status: degraded` so that Railway does not restart the process on stale data alone, since stale data is a user-visible problem and not a process problem.

A Dockerfile fallback is documented for the case where Nixpacks proves insufficient — typically when a system dependency we need is unavailable in Nixpacks' Nix package set. The fallback base is `mcr.microsoft.com/playwright/python:v1.48.0-jammy`, which ships with Chromium and all the runtime libraries already installed and tested by the Playwright maintainers; the only cost is image size, which is acceptable. Deploy command: `railway up` from the repo root.

**Cloudflare egress risk (open).** The 2026-05-10 live recon found that this environment's GCP egress IP is on Cloudflare's datacenter blocklist for the Clubspark edge — every request to `playtennis.usta.com` returns 403 before any header or cookie is evaluated. Railway publishes its own pool of egress IPs that are also in datacenter ranges, so the same block likely applies to a Railway-hosted worker once we try to run the Clubspark side of the FetchRouter from there. This is tracked as a sub-question under Q-011: before the Phase 4 deploy, the user must confirm Railway egress reaches Clubspark, and if it does not, we either route Clubspark fetches through a small residential-egress relay (a tunnel running on the user's home network) or accept that Clubspark stays deferred until that relay exists. TennisLink — the primary source today — is *not* Cloudflare-fronted and is expected to work from Railway without any of this; this section only blocks the Clubspark plane, not v1 functionality. ADR-005 captures the dual-source design that makes this risk a graceful degradation rather than a deploy blocker.

## 13. Roadmap

The phased plan, from now to a v1 the user can rely on. **Phase 0 — Recon.** *Partially complete (2026-05-10).* Passive recon and a live recon attempt are filed in `RECON.md`; ADR-001 is Accepted as Strategy C with the residential-egress rider; ADR-005 splits the data plane into two sources. **What remains in Phase 0:** Q-011 — the user re-runs `scripts/live_recon.py` from a residential egress so we can capture real Clubspark GraphQL contracts and lift Clubspark out of "deferred". Until then, Phase 1 runs against TennisLink only.

**Phase 1 — Core pipeline.** Split into two parallel tracks per ADR-005.

- **Phase 1a — TennisLink track (in flight).** `TennisLinkClient` in `src/fetch/tennislink_client.py`, parsers for the legacy ASP.NET pages, the multi-source `FetchRouter`, sync CLI under Typer wired to the router. This is the path that produces v1 data today. The success criterion is `usta sync` against the user's account, via TennisLink, producing a populated SQLite database with at least one tournament, one draw, every entry, every player profile (with WTN where TennisLink exposes it), and every match.
- **Phase 1b — Clubspark track (gated on Q-011).** Once a residential recon captures real GraphQL contracts: implement `ClubsparkClient` against the Playwright-resident BrowserContext (Strategy C, ADR-001), parsers for the GraphQL payloads, and lift the `NotImplementedError` stubs. The router already prefers Clubspark for GUID-shaped ids, so the day-1 wiring is a single-file swap.

**Phase 2 — Intelligence.** Head-to-head, form windows, strength-of-draw, the Elo-style expected-outcome rating, opponent scouting cards as data structures (UI to follow).

**Phase 3 — UI.** FastAPI routes, Jinja2 templates, the page list from Section 8. The success criterion for Phase 3 is the pre-tournament briefing page.

**Phase 4 — Hardening.** Schema-drift canary, anonymized fixture set, the full test pyramid, Railway deploy with persistent volume, the `/health` endpoint and the runbook.

**Phase 5 — Optional.** UTR cross-reference if obtainable, lightweight ML for serve/return inferences from publicly available match logs, mobile-friendly UI polish, multi-user mode for sharing dashboards with coach and parent through scoped URLs.

## 14. Definition of Done v1

A concrete checklist that defines v1. The user can run a single command and have the dashboard show every tournament they are entered in, every draw, every opponent's scouting card with current singles and doubles WTN. The raw cache holds every fetched response, hashed by request signature. The dashboard is deployed on Railway with a persistent volume and survives a redeploy without data loss. The full test pyramid passes — unit, integration, smoke. `SPEC.md`, `AGENTS.md`, and `RECON.md` reflect the actual built system, not an aspirational version of it. The schema-drift canary runs nightly. The user has used the dashboard to prepare for at least one real tournament and has signed off that the briefing was complete and correct.

## 15. Engineering Standards

Python 3.11 or newer, mypy in strict mode, ruff for linting and formatting (ruff format replaces black). Pydantic v2 for all entity models; httpx for HTTP; playwright for browser automation; sqlite-utils as the default storage layer with SQLAlchemy 2.x available if relational queries get hairy (recorded in ADR-002 once decided). Loguru for logging, with structured fields. Typer for the CLI. Pytest for tests, with hypothesis for property tests on the score parser. Jinja2 for templates.

Commit messages follow conventional-commits style (`feat:`, `fix:`, `chore:`, `docs:`, `refactor:`, `test:`, with optional scope). PRs require passing CI: lint, type, unit, integration smoke. Secrets are environment variables, never committed; `.env.example` is tracked and lists every required variable with a placeholder, `.env` is gitignored. The repo root contains `pyproject.toml` (deps and tool config), `Procfile` (Railway processes), `nixpacks.toml` or `railway.toml` (Railway build), `.env.example`, and the markdown state files enumerated in Section 3.

## 16. Glossary

**USTA** — United States Tennis Association, the national governing body for tennis in the US, operates `playtennis.usta.com`.
**WTN** — World Tennis Number, a 1-to-40 player rating jointly administered by the ITF and national federations including the USTA. Lower is stronger. Singles and doubles WTN are tracked separately and may differ.
**Draw** — the bracket or schedule for a single division within a tournament.
**Seed** — a ranking-based ordering of selected entrants in a draw, used to keep top players from meeting in early rounds.
**Round of N** (R128, R64, R32, R16) — elimination rounds with N players remaining at the start. QF, SF, F are quarterfinals, semifinals, final.
**Walkover** — a match awarded without play because one side did not appear or withdrew before the match began. Distinct from a retirement, which occurs after play has started.
**Retirement** — a match that started but ended early because one player could not continue.
**Sanction** — formal recognition by a governing body that a tournament counts toward rankings.
**Section / District** — USTA's geographic subdivisions; sections are the larger unit (e.g., Southern, Mid-Atlantic), districts are subdivisions within a section.
**ITF** — International Tennis Federation, joint operator of WTN, governing body above the national federations.
**UTR** — Universal Tennis Rating, an independent commercial rating system that competes with WTN; potentially cross-referenceable in Phase 5.
**ADR** — Architecture Decision Record, a numbered, dated, append-only entry in `DECISIONS.md` capturing a durable design choice and its rationale.
**Raw cache** — the on-disk store under `data/raw/` of every unparsed fetch response, keyed by a hash of `(endpoint, params)`, treated as the canonical source of truth from which all derived state can be rebuilt.
**Schema drift** — the situation where the USTA portal's response shape changes in a way that breaks parsers; detected nightly by the canary in Section 9 and surfaced via `QUESTIONS.md`.
